# 02 — The Seven Models

Press keys `1`–`7` to switch models. Each model isolates one idea from orbital
mechanics so you can *see* it. This page gives the physics, the equations, what
appears on screen, and the numbers actually used in the code.

> **Screen-space scaling.** Real orbits use enormous numbers (metres, seconds,
> kilograms). Here every quantity is chosen so the picture *looks* right and fits
> the screen. Distances are in "world units", and the gravitational parameter
> $\mu = GM$ is set to small convenient values. The **physics is real**; only the
> units are made up. Where a model scales a value for display, it is called out.

---

## Model 1 — Gravity-gradient satellite

**Concept.** A satellite is not a point; it has an orientation. Near a planet, the
slight difference in gravity across the satellite's length (the *gravity
gradient*, a tidal effect) produces a torque that aligns a long, dumbbell-shaped
satellite with the local vertical ("nadir-pointing"). Zajac's 1963 film simulated
exactly this attitude behaviour.

**Equations.** The satellite follows a Kepler ellipse

$$r(\nu) = \frac{a(1-e^2)}{1 + e\cos\nu},$$

here with semi-major axis $a = 4$ and eccentricity $e = 0.5$. In the app the
ellipse is generated by sweeping the eccentric anomaly (doc 05); the gravity
vector always points at the centre,

$$\hat{g} = -\frac{\vec{r}}{\lvert \vec{r}\rvert}.$$

**On screen.** A wireframe globe of radius 1 at the origin, the full elliptical
orbit path, and a small box (the satellite) whose long axis points to the centre,
with a line drawn from the satellite to the planet.

**Code.** `draw_gravity_gradient` (`src/main.rs:465`); orbit `orbit_path(4.0, 0.5, 128)`.

## Model 2 — Binary star system

**Concept.** Two stars of different mass orbit their **common centre of mass**,
not each other's centre. The heavier star moves in a smaller circle; both share
one orbital period.

**Equations.** Newton's law of gravitation and the centre-of-mass condition:

$$F = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}, \qquad m_1 r_1 = m_2 r_2, \qquad
T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G(m_1+m_2)}.$$

With masses $m_1:m_2 = 600:200 = 3:1$, the radii are in the inverse ratio
$r_1:r_2 = 1:3$, i.e. $2.5 : 7.5$. The separation is $r = r_1 + r_2 = 10$.

**On screen.** Two spheres (radii 2.2 and 1.4), a small `+` crosshair at the
centre of mass, a line joining them (the action–reaction axis), and two
concentric circular orbit guides.

**Code.** `draw_binary` (`src/main.rs:482`).

## Model 3 — Multi-planet system (Kepler's third law)

**Concept.** Planets on circular orbits: the closer one is, the faster it moves
and the shorter its period. This is the visual content of Kepler's harmonic law.

**Equations.** Balancing gravity with the centripetal requirement gives the
circular speed, and the period follows:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}
= 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}} \;\propto\; r^{3/2}.$$

With radii $4, 9, 16$ the periods are in the ratio $4^{3/2}:9^{3/2}:16^{3/2}
= 8:27:64$.

**Screen scaling.** The code uses $GM = 5000$, so the *physically correct* speeds
would be $\sqrt{5000/r} \approx 35.4,\ 23.6,\ 17.7$. For a comfortable on-screen
speed these are divided by about 10, giving the displayed `3.5, 2.3, 1.7`
(`speeds` in `draw_multi_planet`). The angular rate used for motion is
$\omega = v_{\text{scaled}}/r$. The HUD label shows the un-scaled formula so you
can see where the speed comes from.

**On screen.** A central sphere, three circular orbit rings, three boxes moving
around them, and a live speed label above each planet.

**Code.** `draw_multi_planet` (`src/main.rs:506`).

## Model 4 — Kepler's second law (equal areas)

**Concept.** A planet sweeps out **equal areas in equal times**. It therefore
moves fast at perihelion (closest point) and slow at aphelion (farthest point).

**Equation.** The law is a statement of conservation of angular momentum $L$: the
area swept per unit time is constant,

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{constant}.$$

This model uses $a = 9$, $e = 0.65$, with the Sun at the focus
$\vec{r}_{\text{focus}} = (ae, 0)$.

**On screen.** The ellipse, a sphere at the focus, the moving planet, and shaded
"wedge" sectors drawn every 1.5 s. All wedges have equal area even though their
shapes differ.

**Code.** `draw_areas` (`src/main.rs:541`).

## Model 5 — Orbital energy and escape

**Concept.** The specific orbital energy decides the *shape* of a trajectory:
negative = bound ellipse, zero = parabola, positive = hyperbola (escape).

**Equation.** For a body of speed $v$ at distance $r$ from a mass parameter
$\mu = GM$:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r}.$$

The circular speed is $v_{\text{circ}} = \sqrt{\mu/r}$ and the escape speed is
$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2\mu/r} = \sqrt{2}\,v_{\text{circ}}$.

**Parameters.** $\mu = 100$, all three test bodies start at $r = 4$ so
$v_{\text{circ}} = 5.0$ and $v_{\text{esc}} = 7.07$. Their initial speeds:

| Body | Speed | $\varepsilon$ | Trajectory |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5.8 | < 0 | elliptical (bound) |
| B | 7.07 | = 0 | parabolic (marginal escape) |
| C | 9.2 | > 0 | hyperbolic (escaping) |

**On screen.** A central sphere, three traced trails, and a live energy readout
`E = v²/2 − GM/r` for each body.

**Code.** `draw_energy` (`src/main.rs:580`), readout `energy_readout`
(`src/main.rs:611`). This model advances the motion with a numerical integrator —
see [06 — Numerical Integration](06-numerical-integration.md).

## Model 6 — Geostationary vs. low Earth orbit

**Concept.** A **geostationary** satellite orbits with exactly the same period as
the Earth's rotation, so it hovers over one spot. A **low Earth orbit** (LEO) is
much closer and races around many times in the same period.

**Equation.** Kepler's third law again, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/\mu}$. Geostationary
means $T_{\text{orbit}} = T_{\text{rotation}}$.

**Parameters.** Earth radius 3 (rotating with period 10 units). LEO at
$r = 4.2$ with period 1.5 units; GEO at $r = 10.5$, sharing the Earth's angular
rate so it stays fixed above the surface.

**On screen.** A rotating wireframe Earth with a surface meridian, a near box
(LEO) whipping around, and a far box (GEO) that stays put relative to the ground.

**Code.** `draw_geo_leo` (`src/main.rs:637`).

## Model 7 — Highly eccentric comet

**Concept.** A comet on a very stretched ellipse spends most of its time far away
and then whips rapidly through perihelion.

**Equations.** With $a = 12$, $e = 0.85$:

$$r_p = a(1-e) = 1.8, \qquad r_a = a(1+e) = 22.2, \qquad
\frac{r_a}{r_p} = \frac{1+e}{1-e} = 12.33.$$

The same ratio holds for the speeds, $\frac{v_p}{v_a} = \frac{1+e}{1-e} = 12.33$
(from the vis-viva equation, doc 04).

**On screen.** The long ellipse, a sphere at the focus (the Sun), the comet, and
a tail that points away from the Sun and grows when the comet is fast.

**Code.** `draw_comet` (`src/main.rs:682`).
